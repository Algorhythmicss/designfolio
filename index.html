Leetify

Product — a Chrome extension for Codeforces · 20,000 users · rated 4.3 on the Chrome Web Store

One problem, one page.
Statement, editor, tests and submit, without the tab juggling.

How do you fix the most annoying part of Codeforces without touching the site people love?

  • Redesign the flow, not the site. Only the part with the friction changed: reading, coding, testing and submitting now happen on one page.
  • Make it familiar on purpose. The layout borrows what competitive programmers already know from LeetCode, so there was nothing new to learn and the idea fit in a reel.
  • Help people improve, not just solve. Notes attach to problems, and the training panel explains why you’re not at the next rating yet.

Elsewhere

Festival website — programme filters, pass comparison and a demo checkout

A festival you can get lost in.
A programme and booking flow you won’t.

How do you create a sense of escape without making the practical information hard to find?

  • Let the place set the mood. Full-bleed city collages make the page a small unfamiliar world; short, concrete copy says what the festival is.
  • Help people find their version of the day. Programme filters by day and type, every artist leads to the right day pass, and travel and access are there when needed.
  • Keep the booking decision clear. Day and weekend passes side by side, with guest count and total visible before the demo ticket.

Second Nature

Studio website — a scroll-driven before/after and a brief builder

See an old space differently.
Then start a useful conversation.

How can a studio make an unfamiliar design process feel tangible before a prospective client gets in touch?

  • Make the change visible. One room transforms as you scroll: the architecture stays, the furniture changes, with direct controls as well.
  • Explain what was considered. Two project studies connect the existing space to a proposal, with drawings and collected objects instead of an image grid.
  • Give the conversation a starting point. The enquiry asks about the place, the change, budget and timing, then drafts a brief you can edit.

Side Note

Coffee shop — a three-question finder, product configurator and bag

Find a coffee that fits your morning.
Choose it, grind it, make it yours.

How do you make a specialist product feel approachable without taking away its character?

  • Start with the familiar ritual. Warm colour, printed-paper artwork and plain-language tasting notes make a specialist product friendly.
  • Translate preferences into a choice. A three-question finder uses equipment, milk and taste to explain its pick and set the grind.
  • Make the order understandable. Size, grind and one-off versus repeat are explicit, and today’s total is kept apart from any recurring total.

How we’ll work together.

  1. A conversation. What the product does, who it’s for and what success looks like. You’ll get a plain estimate before anything starts.
  2. Flows and a direction. The key screens and a visual direction, shared early, usually within the first week, so we correct course while it’s cheap.
  3. The build. Real frontend and backend, shipped in small pieces you can click through and react to, not a big reveal at the end.
  4. Launch, then after. We watch how people actually use it, fix what they stumble on, and keep going.

Most projects run two to six weeks. I’m based in Kanpur, India, and work remotely with teams anywhere.
